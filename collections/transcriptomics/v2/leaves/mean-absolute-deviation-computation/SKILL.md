# mean-absolute-deviation-computation

## Summary

Compute the per-gene mean absolute deviation (MAD) between descriptive statistics (median, interquartile range, coefficient of variation, proportion of zero counts) of synthetic scRNA-seq data generated by scDBM or scVI (prior/posterior) and the original dataset, quantifying how faithfully deep generative models trained on small pilot samples (384–2304 cells) reproduce univariate gene distributions.

## When to use

Use when you need a scalar, per-gene measure of how well synthetic scRNA-seq data from a generative model matches the univariate expression distributions of an original dataset, especially to expose failures such as unreproduced bimodal genes like CD74 in PBMC4k.

## When NOT to use

- Input is raw expression data with no synthetic dataset to compare against — MAD comparison requires a paired synthetic counterpart generated from a trained model.
- Goal is batch-effect assessment or batch correction evaluation — the paper explicitly excluded batch effects from its scope and examined all models without batch correction.
- Quality of interest is clustering similarity (ARI, Davies–Bouldin index) or cluster proportions rather than per-gene univariate distributions — other evaluation steps of the paper cover those.

## Inputs

- Original scRNA-seq count matrices (PBMC4k, Segerstolpe)
- Synthetic count matrices generated by scDBM via Gibbs sampling
- Synthetic count matrices generated by scVI from the prior distribution
- Synthetic count matrices generated by scVI from the posterior distribution
- Pilot sub-sampling replicate metadata (30 replicates per pilot size: 384, 768, 1152, 1536, 1920, 2304 cells)

## Outputs

- res_mad_descriptive_stats.csv — aggregated MAD values per gene, ordered by model, pilot size, and metric (median, IQR, coefficient of variation, zero proportion)
- Marginal univariate distribution plots for exemplary genes (e.g., CD74 highlighting bimodality failure, saved as res_bimodality_cd74.png)

## How to apply

Load the original count matrices (e.g., PBMC4k and Segerstolpe) and the synthetic datasets generated by scDBM, scVI prior, and scVI posterior for each pilot size (384, 768, 1152, 1536, 1920, 2304 cells). For each gene in both original and synthetic data compute four descriptive statistics: the median, the interquartile range, the coefficient of variation, and the proportion of zero counts. For each statistic, calculate the mean absolute deviation per gene between synthetic and original values, aggregating over the 30 sub-sampling replicates per pilot size. Store the aggregated MAD values in a results table (res_mad_descriptive_stats.csv) ordered by model, pilot size, and metric. This MAD aggregation is what makes distributional failures such as the bimodality of CD74 visible, since a high MAD on one statistic signals that the model did not learn that aspect of the gene's univariate distribution.

## Examples

```
mad_medians = mean(abs.(median(synth, dims=2) .- median(orig, dims=2)))  # Julia: per-gene MAD of medians, repeated across the 30 sub-sampling replicates per pilot size
```

## Evaluation signals

- res_mad_descriptive_stats.csv contains all four metrics (median, interquartile range, coefficient of variation, zero proportion) for the three models (scDBM, scVI prior, scVI posterior) across all six pilot sizes.
- MAD values are aggregated over the 30 sub-sampling replicates per pilot size, not computed on a single replicate.
- res_bimodality_cd74.png shows that synthetic CD74 distributions fail to capture the bimodal pattern present in the original PBMC4k data, consistent with Supplementary Fig. 3.
- The table rows are ordered by model, pilot size, and metric, matching the analysis dimensions.

## Limitations

- All approaches learn the univariate distribution of most genes, but bimodal gene distributions (e.g., CD74 in PBMC4k) are frequently not reproduced, so low MAD on most genes does not imply distributional fidelity for all genes.
- Evaluation of synthetic data quality from generative models is difficult, with sometimes ambiguous results when based on multiple quality measures.
- For sparser 10x Genomics datasets, inference from small to larger datasets is more challenging than for Smart-seq2 data, so MAD results from Segerstolpe may not transfer to sparser technologies.
