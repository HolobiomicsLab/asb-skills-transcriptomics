# synthetic-data-distribution-comparison

## Summary

Evaluates whether synthetic scRNA-seq datasets generated by scDBM and scVI (prior/posterior variants) trained on small pilot sub-samples faithfully reproduce per-gene univariate distributions of the original data, using per-gene descriptive statistics and exemplary marginal distribution plots.

## When to use

Use when comparing synthetic single-cell RNA-seq data generated by deep generative models (scDBM, scVI) against original count matrices to assess per-gene distribution fidelity, including detecting bimodality failures.

## When NOT to use

- The goal is assessing clustering quality (ARI, Davies–Bouldin index) or cell-type proportions rather than per-gene univariate distribution fidelity
- The synthetic data are a negative-binomial noise baseline (gamma-drawn shape/scale parameters) rather than model-generated data whose gene-distribution fidelity you want to quantify
- Investigating batch effects or batch correction — the paper explicitly excluded batch correction methods from scope

## Inputs

- Original scRNA-seq count matrices (PBMC4k, Segerstolpe)
- Synthetic count matrices from scDBM trained on pilot sub-samples (30 replicates per pilot size: 384–2304 cells)
- Synthetic count matrices from scVI sampled from the prior distribution
- Synthetic count matrices from scVI sampled from the posterior distribution
- Gene names files (e.g., segerstolpe_gene_names.csv)

## Outputs

- res_mad_descriptive_stats.csv — per-gene MAD values for median, interquartile range, coefficient of variation, and zero-count proportion, ordered by model, pilot size, and metric
- res_bimodality_cd74.png — marginal distribution comparison of CD74 original vs. synthetic data from each model

## How to apply

Load the original count matrices (e.g., PBMC4k from 10x Genomics and Segerstolpe from Smart-seq2) together with the synthetic datasets from scDBM (sampled via Gibbs sampling), scVI prior, and scVI posterior for each pilot size (384, 768, 1152, 1536, 1920, 2304 cells). For each gene in both original and synthetic data, compute four descriptive statistics: median, interquartile range, coefficient of variation, and proportion of zero counts. Calculate the mean absolute deviation (MAD) per gene for each statistic between synthetic and original data, aggregating over the 30 sub-sampling replicates per pilot size, and store results ordered by model, pilot size, and metric (res_mad_descriptive_stats.csv). The rationale is that a generative model useful for scRNA-seq study design should preserve univariate gene properties such as zero-inflation and variability; systematic MAD inflation reveals distorted gene distributions. As a qualitative check, plot marginal univariate distributions of exemplary genes (e.g., CD74 from PBMC4k) comparing original against synthetic data, highlighting the known failure to reproduce bimodality (res_bimodality_cd74.png, consistent with Supplementary Fig. 3).

## Evaluation signals

- res_mad_descriptive_stats.csv contains all four metrics (median, IQR, CV, zero-count proportion) for the three models (scDBM, scVI prior, scVI posterior) across all six pilot sizes
- MAD values are aggregated over the 30 sub-sampling replicates per pilot size, so each entry reflects a mean over replicates
- res_bimodality_cd74.png shows that synthetic distributions of CD74 fail to capture the bimodal pattern present in the original PBMC4k data, matching the paper's Supplementary Fig. 3
- The synthetic datasets compared are generated at the size of the original study from pilot datasets of 384, 768, 1152, 1536, 1920, and 2304 cells
- Median, IQR, CV, and zero fraction are computed per gene on both original and synthetic matrices before taking absolute deviations

## Limitations

- All approaches learn the univariate distribution of most genes but frequently fail to reproduce bimodal gene distributions (exemplified by CD74 in PBMC4k)
- For 10x Genomics datasets with higher sparsity, it is more challenging to infer from small to larger datasets than for Smart-seq2 data
- The paper examined all models without using potentially available batch correction capabilities, and scDBMs currently cannot learn batch effects
- Evaluating synthetic data quality is difficult because examination based on multiple quality measures can be ambiguous
