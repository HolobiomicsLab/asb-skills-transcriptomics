# clustering-quality-metric-tabulation

## Summary

Tabulates clustering-quality metrics (Davies–Bouldin index, adjusted Rand index, cluster-proportion differences) for synthetic scRNA-seq data generated from small pilot sub-samples, enabling technology-stratified comparisons (10x Genomics vs Smart-seq2) across pilot sizes of 384–2304 cells.

## When to use

Decision support for evaluating whether synthetic single-cell RNA-seq data from small pilot studies preserve clustering structure — e.g., when comparing DBI/ARI variability of scDBM, scVI prior, scVI posterior, and noise baselines across pilot sizes or technologies.

## When NOT to use

- When batch effects are present and batch correction matters — the study examined all models without using potentially available batch correction capabilities, and scDBMs currently cannot learn batch effects
- When the goal is assessing per-gene distributional fidelity (medians, IQRs, coefficients of variation, zero-count proportions) rather than clustering-quality metrics — that requires a separate evaluation workflow
- When the pilot sample is not representative of the target data (experimental settings changed), since conclusions from small to larger datasets hinge on representativeness

## Inputs

- Count matrices for PBMC4k (10x Genomics) and Segerstolpe (Smart-seq2) with gene names and Seurat cluster labels (segerstolpe_gene_names.csv, segerstolpe_hvg_clustering.csv)
- 30 random sub-samples per pilot size (384, 768, 1152, 1536, 1920, 2304 cells)
- Synthetic datasets generated by scDBM (Gibbs sampling), scVI prior, scVI posterior, and negative binomial noise baseline
- UMAP embeddings of original and synthetic observations

## Outputs

- Per-run DBI and ARI values for each generative model, dataset, and pilot size (as visualized in Figure 2)
- Mean absolute differences in cell numbers per cluster between original and synthetic data (Table 1)
- Tidy stratified CSV, e.g. technology_comparison.csv with columns technology, dataset, model, metric, pilot_size, value

## How to apply

Extract sub-samples of 384, 768, 1152, 1536, 1920, and 2304 cells (30 sub-samples each) from the original datasets (PBMC4k from 10x Genomics; Segerstolpe from Smart-seq2), train scDBM (sampled via Gibbs) and scVI (prior and posterior variants), plus a negative binomial noise baseline with gamma-drawn scale and shape parameters from Uniform(0,5) and Uniform(0,10), and generate synthetic data at the size of the original study. Apply UMAP dimensionality reduction and Seurat clustering to the original data, then assign cluster labels to synthetic observations by mapping to the closest original observation based on Euclidean distance in UMAP space. Compute the Davies–Bouldin index (DBI) and adjusted Rand index (ARI) per synthetic dataset, and the mean of absolute differences in cell numbers per cluster between original and synthetic data. Assemble a tidy stratified table (e.g., technology_comparison.csv) with columns for technology, dataset, generative model, metric, and value per pilot size, matching the paper's Figure 2 trends and Table 1 numbers. Correct application shows the scVI posterior variant with high DBI variability on the sparser PBMC4k data and tighter, more reference-close DBI distributions for the Smart-seq2 Segerstolpe data.

## Evaluation signals

- Tabulated DBI/ARI values reproduce the qualitative trends of Figure 2: higher variability for the scVI posterior variant on PBMC4k, tighter DBI distributions closer to the original-data reference on Segerstolpe
- Cluster-proportion differences in the table match the paper's reported Table 1 numbers (Segerstolpe covers only 1-5 plates)
- All runs use the specified grid: 30 sub-samples at each of the six pilot sizes (384–2304 cells) for both datasets
- The noise baseline (negative binomial with Uniform(0,5) scale and Uniform(0,10) shape) appears as a distinct model column in the tidy table
- Output CSV validates against the tidy schema: technology, dataset, generative model, metric, and value per pilot size

## Limitations

- The scVI posterior variant showed high variability in clustering similarity, most likely due to amplifying artifacts of small datasets
- All approaches overrepresented highly abundant cell types and missed less abundant cell types
- Evaluating synthetic data quality is difficult, with sometimes ambiguous examination based on multiple quality measures
- The approach relies on the unverified assumption that synthetic cells mapped to the original data are mapped to the correct clusters
